The strongest blog posts for interviews do not try to prove that you know
everything. They prove that you can **notice a real problem, make tradeoffs, and
explain why a solution worked**.

## What interviewers actually get from a post

When an interviewer opens a technical article, they are scanning for three
signals:

1. Can you frame a problem clearly?
2. Can you show how you chose between competing options?
3. Can you describe the result without hiding the rough edges?

That means the best posts are rarely generic tutorials. They are usually
grounded in a concrete decision:

- a debugging session that surfaced the real bottleneck
- a system design choice under constraints
- a refactor that traded speed for maintainability
- an evaluation pipeline that turned out to be misleading

## A structure that works

Use a simple progression:

1. Background: What was the situation and what constraint mattered?
2. Failure mode: What broke, slowed down, or became ambiguous?
3. Options: What approaches were on the table?
4. Decision: Why did you choose one path?
5. Result: What changed after the fix?
6. Reflection: What would you do differently next time?

This shape is readable in five minutes, which matters. Nobody interviewing you
is going to spend half an hour on a single article.

## Details that carry disproportionate weight

A few specifics make a post feel credible very quickly:

- real before-and-after numbers
- the exact symptom that pointed toward the root cause
- one rejected approach and why it was rejected
- one limitation that still remains

These details communicate engineering judgment. They also give an interviewer
better follow-up questions than a polished summary ever could.

## What to avoid

Avoid posts that only describe implementation steps. A post that says _first we
used tool A, then service B, then wrote script C_ sounds busy but does not show
thinking.

Avoid sweeping claims too:

> This improved the system significantly.

That line is weak unless you can say **how** it improved the system and **what
tradeoff came with it**.

## A strong finish

End with a short conclusion that says one thing clearly:

- what changed
- what you learned
- what remains open

That ending is often the part an interviewer remembers most. It tells them
whether you treat engineering as a sequence of tools or as a process of making
defensible decisions.
